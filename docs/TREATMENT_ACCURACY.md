# Casualty Clearers App — Treatment Accuracy and Content Decisions

Current-source review completed 11 September 2026

## Purpose

This document records which casualty-clearer subjects can be written from current guidance now, which subjects belong in the information hub but still need an exact AOPS or equipment source, and which older practices should not be carried forward. It is a content-control document for the consolidation project. It does not add course scheduling, administration, patient records, or a web-app build.

## Bottom line

Most of the intended content remains useful. The main changes are not about removing the practical AOPS material; they are about preventing older medical wording from being copied into the consolidated content. Current national and international sources support direct pressure, trained wound packing, commercial tourniquets, CPR/AED, basic airway manoeuvres, careful splinting, burn cooling, hypothermia care, and simple monitoring. The AOPS workflows—muster, team organization, communications, movement routes, Sick Bay support, firefighter rehabilitation, person-overboard recovery, and equipment carriage—remain local operational content.

Several items need correction or tighter boundaries before app-ready pages are drafted:

Choking: the current 2025 Heart & Stroke sequence for a responsive adult with severe foreign-body airway obstruction is five back blows followed by five abdominal thrusts, repeated until the object is expelled or the person becomes unresponsive. Older “abdominal thrusts first” wording should not be used. [1]

Infant CPR: the 2025 resuscitation update no longer recommends the traditional two-finger compression technique. Current training materials use the heel of one hand or the two-thumb encircling technique, depending on rescuer position and ability. [1]

Oxygen: oxygen should not be presented as an automatic treatment for every casualty. Current first-aid evidence does not establish a general benefit, identifies no benefit for routine oxygen in acute stroke, and warns against driving oxygen saturation above 92% in a person with known COPD. In this project, casualty clearers should learn safe setup and operation, but actual administration targets and indications need ship medical direction or the applicable trained protocol. [3]

Spinal equipment: rigid collars, KEDs, and long boards should not be described as routine “spinal immobilization” treatment. Current first-aid guidance recommends keeping a suspected spinal-injury casualty still unless movement is necessary and recommends against routine collar and long-board use by first-aid providers. On AOPS, these devices may still have a valid locally directed packaging, extraction, and movement role; that role must be stated accurately. [3]

Open chest wounds: do not carry forward generic advice to seal every open chest wound with an unvented occlusive dressing. Current first-aid guidance permits leaving the wound exposed, using a clean nonocclusive dry dressing, or using a purpose-designed vented chest seal, with close monitoring and loosening or removal if breathing worsens. [3]

Burns: immediate clean running-water cooling is strongly supported. The current Canadian Red Cross guide says at least 10 minutes; the broader 2024 AHA/Red Cross guideline says 5–20 minutes may be reasonable, and ILCOR says the evidence does not identify one optimal duration. The app should use the current Canadian baseline and record the evidence difference rather than pretending all authorities give an identical number. [3][4][5]

Monitoring: pulse oximetry and Rad-57 readings must remain part of the whole assessment and should never replace observed breathing, skin signs, history, or changes over time. The Rad-57 manufacturer specifically says SpCO must not be the sole basis for diagnosis or treatment decisions about suspected carbon-monoxide poisoning. [3][9]

## Status key

CONFIRMED — Current authoritative guidance supports the core content. App-ready wording can be drafted, while still matching local terminology.

LOCAL / MODEL CHECK — The subject belongs in the hub, but exact instructions depend on an AOPS procedure, the medical team’s direction, or the exact equipment model.

REVISE / EXCLUDE — Older wording should not be carried forward, or the subject is outside the agreed scope.

## 1. Assessment and organization

### Scene safety, PPE, and immediate hazards — CONFIRMED

Retain scene size-up, PPE, hazard recognition, and the rule that a casualty clearer does not enter an unsafe or contaminated area without the required training and protection. Shipboard fire, smoke, electrical, flooding, and hazardous-material conditions should be described using AOPS procedures, not generic shore-based assumptions.

### MARCHE as the main navigation path — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Retain MARCHE as the organizing path because it reflects the ship’s training and casualty-clearing workflow. MARCHE itself is not the source of every treatment instruction. Each intervention linked from MARCHE must use the current treatment decision recorded below. Avoid expanding it into clinical decision-making beyond casualty-clearer training.

### AVPU, vital signs, secondary survey, and reassessment — CONFIRMED

Retain AVPU, breathing rate and quality, pulse, skin findings, capillary refill where locally taught, manual blood pressure, and repeated reassessment. Record trends and changes rather than implying that one number establishes a diagnosis. A physical assessment and history remain primary; monitor values are supporting information. [3][4]

### MIST, SITREP, traumagram, and communications — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Retain as communication and documentation subjects. Use the exact AOPS format and terminology. These pages should help casualty clearers collect and pass information, not interpret findings as clinicians. Do not add patient-storage or administrative functionality to the future app.

## 2. Massive hemorrhage

### Direct pressure — CONFIRMED

Use firm direct manual pressure as the first method for initial control of severe external bleeding. Expose the bleeding site when practicable, maintain pressure, and reassess effectiveness. [3][4]

### Pressure dressing — CONFIRMED

Once bleeding is controlled, a pressure dressing may be used to maintain control. The page should emphasize reassessment for recurrent bleeding and should not substitute a pressure dressing for hands-on pressure before control is achieved. [3][4]

### Wound packing — CONFIRMED, IF TRAINED

Keep wound packing for trained casualty clearers. It is appropriate for severe bleeding where the wound location can be packed, especially when a tourniquet cannot be used. Gauze or hemostatic gauze is packed to the bleeding site and followed by firm direct pressure. Exact hands-on technique should match the course and the carried product. [3][6]

### Tourniquet — CONFIRMED, IF TRAINED

Use a manufactured commercial tourniquet for life-threatening extremity bleeding when direct pressure does not control the bleeding, cannot be maintained, or is impractical in the circumstances. Tighten until bleeding stops, secure the device, record the application time, and reassess. A casualty clearer should not remove an applied tourniquet. [4][5]

Placement language needs one deliberate choice. The current Canadian Red Cross guide places a tourniquet 5–10 cm above the injury, moving above a joint when necessary. Tactical “high and tight” language may appear in older or threat-based material. Do not combine those phrases on one generic page. Confirm the exact local course wording and the manufacturer’s directions, then state the selected method consistently. [4]

### Pressure points — REVISE / EXCLUDE AS A PRIMARY METHOD

Do not teach arterial pressure points as a preferred hemorrhage-control method. Current evidence finds their utility uncertain and shows that distal flow can return quickly. [3]

## 3. Airway

### Basic airway opening — CONFIRMED

For an unresponsive person, use a head-tilt/chin-lift when appropriate. With suspected head or neck trauma, trained rescuers may start with a jaw thrust; if the airway cannot be opened, airway and ventilation take priority and a head-tilt/chin-lift is used. [1][4]

### OPA — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Keep OPA because it is an individually performed shipboard skill. Use only for the unresponsive casualty without an intact gag response, select the correct size, stop if gagging occurs, and continue to maintain and monitor the airway after insertion. The available Canadian Red Cross professional-responder manual supports the general technique, but the final quick page should be checked against the current AOPS training method before release. [8]

### NPA — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Keep NPA because it is an individually performed shipboard skill. Do not publish a generic insertion card until the current local training method, contraindications, lubrication method, and sizing technique are confirmed. The information hub should not silently merge procedures from different courses.

### BVM ventilation — CONFIRMED CORE; LOCAL / MODEL CHECK FOR THE CARD

Retain BVM use by trained casualty clearers. The core treatment points are an open airway, an effective mask seal, ventilation only to visible chest rise, and avoidance of excessive rate or volume. Two-rescuer BVM technique is preferable when trained help is available. For an adult with respiratory arrest and a pulse, current 2025 guidance supports one ventilation every six seconds for a health-care-trained rescuer. During adult CPR before an advanced airway, use 30 compressions to two breaths. The final card must match the BVM type and the casualty-clearer course. [1][8]

### Suction — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Include only if it is actually carried and taught to casualty clearers. If not confirmed, omit rather than expanding scope.

## 4. CPR, AED, and choking

### Adult CPR — CONFIRMED

Use 30 compressions and two breaths for trained rescuers before an advanced airway. Compress at 100–120 per minute, at least 5 cm but avoid going deeper than 6 cm, allow full recoil, minimize pauses, and give each breath over about one second only until the chest rises. If breaths cannot or will not be given, compression-only CPR is better than no CPR. Breaths are especially important in drowning, choking, and other breathing-related arrests. [1][2][4]

### AED — CONFIRMED

Turn on the AED, expose and dry the chest, apply pads according to their diagrams, keep everyone clear during analysis and shock, and resume compressions immediately after a shock or a no-shock decision. Continue following prompts. Use the actual shipboard AED model for button names, status checks, pad locations, and any reporting or replenishment steps. [4]

### Adult severe choking — REVISE TO THE 2025 SEQUENCE

Use repeated cycles of five back blows followed by five abdominal thrusts. Use chest thrusts in late pregnancy or when the abdomen cannot be encircled. If the casualty becomes unresponsive, begin CPR with compressions and look for a visible object before breaths; do not perform blind finger sweeps. [1]

### Child and infant choking — REVISE TO THE 2025 SEQUENCE

For a child with severe obstruction, use five back blows followed by five abdominal thrusts. For an infant, use five back blows followed by five chest thrusts. If unresponsive, transition to the applicable CPR algorithm. [1]

Pediatric and infant CPR — LOCAL TRAINING UPDATE REQUIRED

The future content should match the current 2025 Heart & Stroke course materials rather than relying only on the Canadian Red Cross comprehensive guide, which may reflect the pre-October-2025 infant hand-position wording. Specifically, do not teach the older two-finger infant compression technique as the current standard. [1][4]

## 5. Breathing, chest injury, smoke, and carbon monoxide

### Breathing difficulty — CONFIRMED

Allow an awake casualty with breathing difficulty to use the most comfortable position, usually sitting upright. Observe rate, effort, sounds, ability to speak, skin signs, and change over time. Escalate immediately through the ship’s response chain. [3][4]

### Open chest wound — REVISE

If this subject is added, use current first-aid wording: activate the emergency response, leave exposed or cover with a clean nonocclusive dry dressing, or use a purpose-designed vented chest seal if trained. Monitor continuously. If breathing worsens after a dressing or seal is applied, loosen or remove it. Do not describe an improvised fully occlusive seal as universally safe. [3]

### Smoke inhalation — CONFIRMED CORE; LOCAL TREATMENT CHECK

Retain immediate removal from the hazardous atmosphere only when it can be done safely, airway and breathing assessment, early reporting to the medical team, and close monitoring. Facial burns, breathing difficulty, singed nasal hair, or soot around the nose or mouth indicate possible inhalation injury and require urgent medical response. [3][4]

### Carbon monoxide — CONFIRMED CORE; LOCAL TREATMENT CHECK

Recognize headache, dizziness, confusion, weakness, nausea, chest symptoms, altered responsiveness, and exposure history. Move to fresh air without entering an unsafe atmosphere. Do not rely on an ordinary pulse-oximeter reading to rule out carbon-monoxide poisoning. If Rad-57 is used, record and report the SpCO result with symptoms and circumstances; do not let the number independently determine treatment. [4][9]

## 6. Shock

### General care — CONFIRMED

Control the cause when possible, keep the casualty at a normal temperature, monitor airway and breathing, maintain a position appropriate to the casualty’s condition, and continue reassessment. Do not give food or drink to a casualty with suspected internal bleeding or moderate/severe hypothermia. [3][4]

### Position — REVISE IF OLDER MATERIAL ROUTINELY RAISES THE LEGS

For an alert casualty with shock, supine is reasonable. Leg elevation is only a possible option when there is no trauma or injury and it does not worsen symptoms. It should not be a routine trauma instruction. Use the recovery position when decreased alertness creates an airway risk and the casualty is breathing normally, unless trauma makes movement inappropriate. [3]

## 7. Burns and chemical exposure

### Thermal burns — CONFIRMED WITH A RECORDED SOURCE DIFFERENCE

Stop the burning process. Cool promptly with clean running water. For a Canadian baseline, use at least 10 minutes from the current Canadian Red Cross guide. Record that the 2024 AHA/Red Cross guideline considers 5–20 minutes reasonable and ILCOR does not identify one optimal duration. Prevent whole-body cooling and hypothermia, especially with large burns or children. Remove jewelry and loose clothing, but do not pull away material stuck to the burn. After cooling, cover loosely with a clean dry nonadherent dressing. Do not break blisters and do not put ointment on significant partial- or full-thickness burns while awaiting medical care. [3][4][5]

### Electrical burns — CONFIRMED CORE

Make the electrical source safe before contact, monitor airway, breathing, and circulation, consider associated trauma, and obtain urgent medical support. Use local shipboard electrical-isolation procedures.

### Chemical skin exposure — CONFIRMED CORE; LOCAL HAZMAT PROCESS CONTROLS

Protect the responder and prevent spread. Remove contaminated clothing and jewelry. Brush off dry chemicals before irrigation unless the chemical-specific procedure says otherwise. Unless local or chemical-specific information directs differently, irrigate exposed skin with running water for at least 15 minutes. Safety Data Sheets and the ship’s hazardous-material response procedures take priority for chemical-specific exceptions. [3][4]

### Chemical eye exposure — CONFIRMED CORE

Begin irrigation immediately, avoid contaminating the other eye or responders, and use copious tap water for 15 minutes unless local chemical-specific guidance directs otherwise. [3]

## 8. Hypothermia, immersion, and person overboard

### Hypothermia recognition — CONFIRMED

Use observable stages rather than relying on a core temperature casualty clearers may not be able to obtain. Cold stress and mild hypothermia usually involve shivering with declining function; moderate hypothermia may include weak or stopped shivering, poor coordination, slurred speech, confusion, or unresponsiveness; severe hypothermia may include absent shivering, very slow or absent breathing, unresponsiveness, and stiffness. [3][4]

### Hypothermia care — CONFIRMED

Handle gently, keep the casualty horizontal when practicable, remove or isolate wet clothing, insulate from the deck and environment, cover the head and neck, use a vapour barrier where appropriate, and warm the upper trunk with protected heat sources. Monitor warmed skin for burns. For an unresponsive casualty with suspected hypothermia, check breathing for up to 60 seconds. If not breathing, start CPR. Do not provide food or drink in moderate or severe hypothermia. [3][4]

### Person overboard and cold-water recovery — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Retain the cold-shock, cold-incapacitation, hypothermia, and circum-rescue-collapse concepts; the PFD, HELP, and huddle material is useful. The rescue-boat role, recovery method, lifting equipment, handover point, and route into the ship must use AOPS procedures. Treat any cold-water rescue as a possible hypothermia case. Do not add a generic instruction to enter the water without specific rescue training. [4]

## 9. Splinting, spinal care, and casualty movement

### Basic splinting — CONFIRMED

Splint when it reduces pain, prevents further injury, or facilitates necessary movement. Treat a deformed limb in the position found unless straightening is necessary for safe urgent transport and is within the responder’s training. Cover open wounds, control severe bleeding, and check distal circulation, sensation, and movement before and after. Immobilize the joint above and below a long-bone injury or the bones above and below a joint injury. [3][4]

### SAM or mouldable splint — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Include a short use page based on the actual carried splint and local technique. Emphasize padding, position found, secure but not overly tight, and CSM reassessment.

### Sager traction splint — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK; FAMILIARIZATION LEVEL

Keep as a brief familiarization subject, not an independent generic procedure. Current Canadian Red Cross professional-responder content says commercial traction splints are primarily for femur fractures and must be applied only by someone proficient with that model. The manufacturer states that each model has its own application instructions. Confirm the exact Sager model before producing a quick card. [8][11]

### Pelvic binder or pelvic wrap — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

The subject belongs only if the device is carried and casualty clearers are trained or directed to assist. Confirm the exact product, placement landmark, indications, contraindications, and who authorizes application. Do not write a generic procedure by blending different binders.

### Cervical collar, KED, backboard, scoop, Med Sled, and head blocks — REVISE THE PURPOSE

Describe these as locally taught packaging, extraction, and movement tools, not as automatic treatment for every suspected spinal injury. Current first-aid guidance recommends keeping the casualty still unless movement is necessary and recommends against routine rigid-collar and long-board immobilization by first-aid providers. The shipboard movement environment can justify a specific role, but that role must come from AOPS training and the medical team. [3]

### Casualty movement — CONFIRMED PRINCIPLE; LOCAL TECHNIQUE CHECK

Avoid unnecessary movement. Move for immediate danger, access to a higher-priority casualty, or when movement is required to provide care or reach the ship’s casualty-clearing route. Team commands, lifting positions, ladders, hatches, Med Sled routes, rescue-boat transfer, and Sick Bay handover are local AOPS content. [3][4]

### Stokes or basket stretcher — INCLUDE BRIEFLY

Mention as a basket stretcher and retain only a short identification and possible-use note because it is not normally used by this team. Do not give it equal weight with the actual movement equipment.

### Ashton Waters stretcher — EXCLUDE

Do not include it. It is no longer used.

## 10. Oxygen and monitoring equipment

### Oxygen cylinder setup and shutdown — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Include a practical equipment card: identify the cylinder, secure it, inspect the seal and regulator, open it correctly, check pressure and leaks, connect the prescribed delivery device, verify flow, and shut down and depressurize according to the actual regulator instructions. Do not make the equipment card into a generic list of clinical indications. The Canadian Red Cross confirms oxygen-cylinder, regulator, and delivery-device training as current professional-responder content. [7][8]

### Oxygen administration — REVISE

Do not state “give high-flow oxygen” as an automatic response to trauma, smoke, stroke, or any low-looking number. Current first-aid guidance says the overall benefit of oxygen in general first aid is not established, routine oxygen is not beneficial for acute stroke, and excessive oxygen may harm a person with known COPD. The final app wording should say who on AOPS may direct oxygen, what casualty-clearer training authorizes, and what saturation target applies under that protocol. [3]

### Pulse oximetry — CONFIRMED WITH LIMITATIONS

Use the reading as part of the whole assessment. Movement, cold extremities, poor perfusion, ambient light, device quality, nail products, and skin pigmentation can affect accuracy. Recheck questionable numbers, observe the casualty, and report trends. Do not let a number override obvious respiratory distress. [3]

### Rad-57 — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

A short card may cover powering on, selecting the correct sensor, placement, signal-quality confirmation, allowing a stable reading, identifying SpO2, pulse rate, SpCO, and any configured SpMet display, then recording and reporting the reading. It must also state that SpCO is not a stand-alone diagnosis or treatment trigger. Use the current manual for the exact shipboard part ID and sensor. [9]

### Propaq / ZOLL — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Limit the casualty-clearer page to the functions actually assigned: power-on, connecting the taught cuffs or sensors, obtaining the assigned readings, recognizing obvious connection or signal problems, and reporting values. Do not include defibrillation, pacing, advanced ECG interpretation, invasive monitoring, or alarm reconfiguration unless the medical team explicitly assigns those tasks. Use the current 2025 ZOLL Propaq MD operator guide for the exact model and software configuration. [10]

### Manual blood pressure and basic vital signs — CONFIRMED

Retain respirations, pulse, skin, level of responsiveness, capillary refill where locally taught, manual BP, and repeated trending. Use correct cuff sizing and record the time and circumstances of each set.

## 11. Hazardous-material and shipboard special situations

### Environmental spill response — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Use CRR 5-93 and AOPS procedures for zones, PPE, command and control, decontamination flow, casualty movement, and the boundary between life-saving intervention and responder contamination risk. Use current first-aid guidance only for the underlying skin and eye decontamination principles. Do not substitute generic first-aid access for the ship’s controlled-entry rules.

### Firefighter rehabilitation and cooling chairs — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Retain the actual AOPS workflow, assigned vital signs, cooling-chair use, rest and recheck process, and escalation to the medical team. Do not publish generic pass/fail thresholds until the ship’s current procedure or medical direction is identified.

### Isolated casualty response — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Retain the ship-specific dispatch, communication, equipment, extraction, and handover workflow. Link to the confirmed treatment pages rather than restating different versions of the same procedure.

### SMART triage — LOCAL / MODEL CHECK

Retain identification and the locally taught method. Confirm the exact current system, tags, priorities, and terminology before drafting the page. Triage content should organize casualties and communication; it should not expand casualty-clearer treatment scope.

## 12. Training-only and excluded content

### Training IV bag and line priming — KEEP, CLEARLY LABELLED AS TRAINING-ONLY

Include only the taught practice of spiking a training IV bag and priming a line. Do not include venous insertion, medication administration, fluid-selection decisions, or treatment claims.

### Course schedules, attendance, tests, and administration — EXCLUDE

These do not belong in the information hub.

### Patient records or patient-entry features — EXCLUDE

No patient information or operational case data is to be stored.

### Full equipment manuals inside the app — EXCLUDE

Use short practical “how to use” pages. Keep the current manufacturer manual as the behind-the-scenes source for model-specific safety and operating details.

### Unverified illustrations, inherited ChatGPT text, and decorative placeholders — EXCLUDE

Do not copy them merely because they appear in supplied files. Use only content that supports the consolidated information.

## Recommended next work

### 1. Freeze the confirmed general-care content

Draft the app-ready information pages for scene safety, assessment, direct pressure, pressure dressings, trained wound packing, commercial tourniquet principles, basic airway positioning, adult CPR/AED, the updated 2025 choking sequence, shock, thermal and chemical burns, hypothermia, basic splinting, and monitoring limitations. Keep each topic in one canonical page so different sections link to the same wording.

### 2. Run one focused AOPS medical/local validation session

Use the amber items as the agenda: exact tourniquet placement wording, OPA/NPA method, BVM rate and team method, oxygen authorization and targets, chest-seal availability and training, spinal equipment purpose, pelvic-binder responsibility, Sager model and scope, Propaq tasks, Rad-57 sensor and reporting, firefighter-rehabilitation thresholds, SMART triage version, and POB recovery/handover. This is a much smaller task than re-reviewing the whole project.

### 3. Build model-specific equipment cards

For each carried model, create a one-page card with: identify the device; when the casualty clearer uses or assists with it; setup; use; reassessment; common mistakes; stop/escalate points; and post-use steps. Confirm the model first. Priority cards are tourniquet, oxygen regulator and masks, OPA/NPA set, BVM, AED, SAM splint, Propaq/ZOLL, Rad-57, movement devices, and any confirmed pelvic wrap or Sager.

### 4. Consolidate the AOPS workflows around the confirmed pages

Write the ship-specific muster, communications, dispatch, two-person team, casualty movement, Sick Bay return, isolated-casualty, firefighter-rehabilitation, and person-overboard pages. Link them to the canonical treatment and equipment pages rather than duplicating treatment steps.

### 5. Perform a final contradiction check before any build

Search the consolidated content for older choking sequences, two-finger infant CPR, routine oxygen, routine leg elevation for trauma shock, routine collar/backboard immobilization, unvented occlusive chest-seal instructions, pressure-point emphasis, obsolete Ashton Waters references, and conflicting burn-cooling times. Resolve each item explicitly.

### 6. Build only after content decisions are stable

The project is still content consolidation. No web-app construction is included in this step.
